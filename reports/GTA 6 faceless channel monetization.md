# Original commentary, not reposted footage, earns

No lawful route monetizes GTA 6 footage that a faceless channel did not record itself, and the research found no GTA 6 creator program that covers it. Rockstar's only published fan policy tolerates non-commercial posting and defines non-commercial as **not making money through the footage** ([Rockstar Support](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/7bNaeoMFTV0iUDGhStTXvz/policy-on-posting-copyrighted-rockstar-games-material)), which read literally excludes ad-monetized videos. YouTube then judges the whole channel, and its reused-content rule **applies even with the original creator's permission** ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)). Stripping narration and facecams makes the rights position worse, because narration is the creator's main transformative contribution. The viable path is a faceless commentary and analysis channel where the creator's own script and voice carry each video, with only short, purposeful, credited clips. Expect slow, small income: the sourced revenue figures are third-party and conflict with each other, and the 2027 YouTube thresholds are rising.

## Rockstar tolerates non-commercial clips, not monetized ones

Rockstar's fan policy, **last updated 2025-01-10**, says that "Take-Two Interactive does not object to our fans using materials for non-commercial uses in a manner which does not intentionally spoil the plot for others." It also reserves the right to take material down "at any time" and sets out examples that draw takedowns: spoiler compilations, "isolated videos of the game's cinematics," in-game TV or comedy shown in isolation, and footage from leaked copies ([Rockstar Support](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/7bNaeoMFTV0iUDGhStTXvz/policy-on-posting-copyrighted-rockstar-games-material)). The page does not name GTA VI, and no GTA VI-specific creator policy was located as of 2026-11-26 ([bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/games/articles/gta-6-content-creator-rules-spoilers-streaming-and-youtube-monetization), secondary). The Terms of Service are stricter. They limit use to **"your own personal, non-commercial use"** and require "separate, express written terms provided by Rockstar" for commercial exploitation ([Rockstar Legal, s.6.1](https://www.rockstargames.com/legal)). Section 5.3 defines "Custom Content" to include "gameplay clips and livestreams," and section 5.4 says Rockstar "reserves all rights to and ownership" of it. Whether ordinary in-game recording tools fall under that definition is unclear.

Rockstar's only documented monetization channel is its Creator Platform, which is for mods, not videos: it is "the exclusive licensed home for our community to monetize their mods" ([Rockstar Mod Guidelines](https://www.rockstargames.com/community-resources/mod-guidelines)). The stricter wording that advertising or sponsored footage is "presumed to be in violation" appears only in a secondary summary and could not be located on a Rockstar page, so it stays **unverified**. No Rockstar statement on in-game music, commentary or reaction content was found.

## Platforms judge the channel, not the individual clip

YouTube's reused-content rule allows footage when "viewers can tell that there's a meaningful difference," with examples such as critical reviews, reaction videos that comment on the original, and "edited footage from other creators where you add a storyline and commentary." It disallows "short videos you compiled from other social media websites," clips "edited together with little or no narrative," and "content uploaded many times by other creators" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)). The rule "applies to your channel as a whole," so one repost-heavy upload can cost monetization everywhere on the channel. Shorts are stricter: non-original Shorts are ineligible, and **Shorts over one minute that contain claimed content are blocked from monetization** ([YouTube Shorts policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220?hl=en)).

Eligibility is also tightening. The current Partner Program bar is 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 qualified watch hours or 10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days. YouTube announced on 2026-08-10 that **from 1 February 2027, new applicants need 8,000 qualified watch hours in 365 days or 20 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days**, and Shorts revenue sharing requires 10 million qualified Shorts views ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/youtube-partner-program-updates-2027-new-opportunities-earn/)). Whether the 1,000-subscriber minimum survives in the new terms is not stated in the sources read. TikTok's Creator Rewards Program requires 10,000 followers, 100,000 views in 30 days, and videos that are "original" and over one minute. The US FAQ says content must be "filmed, designed, and produced entirely by yourself" ([TikTok Support](https://support.tiktok.com/en/business-and-creator/creator-rewards-program)), a stricter test than the EEA terms' "adds new ideas" wording ([TikTok EEA terms](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/tiktok-creator-rewards-program-eea/en)). Meta's March 2026 rules treat re-uploads and "minor edits... like adding borders, inserting captions, and changing the reel's speed" as unoriginal, and warn that accounts posting mainly unoriginal content "may see their account deemed non-recommendable and demonetized" ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/rewarding-original-creators-on-facebook/)). Instagram's bonus is invite-only in the US, Japan and South Korea, requires 5 million views over three months, and excludes aggregator accounts ([Instagram Help](https://help.instagram.com/708013994693013)).

Content ID is a separate track. A rights holder can block, monetize or track a match ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797370?hl=en)). Secondary sources say a Content ID claim usually does not create a copyright strike, but that is unverified and should be checked in YouTube Studio's Copyright tab. YouTube's pages do not describe how Content ID handles heavy edits or stripped audio. Publisher Content ID behavior for GTA footage was not researched and is the largest open question for a GTA channel.

## Stripping narration and facecams makes the rights position worse

In the US, fair use turns mainly on whether a use is transformative. The Supreme Court's 2023 *Warhol* decision held that where a commercial use shares the original's purpose, the first factor "is likely to weigh against fair use" ([Jenner](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/client-alert-putting-the-use-back-in-fair-use-the-supreme-court-decides-andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith)). The opinion itself was not read, so the wording comes from summaries. The strongest supportive precedent, *Hosseinzadeh v. Klein* (S.D.N.Y. 2017), found a 14-minute reaction video fair use because its commentary was "quintessential criticism and comment," and the court added that it was not ruling that all reaction videos qualify ([US Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/hosseinzadeh-klein-sdny2017.pdf)). A narration-stripped re-cut has no commentary to point to. It is the same footage, in the same entertainment purpose, for profit, which is the fact pattern *Warhol* weighs against.

The rights are layered. The publisher owns the game's audiovisual output, and the original creator owns the cut, pacing and overlays they added. Removing the narration and facecam removes the creator's most obvious contribution but does not clear either layer, so the re-cut remains a copy of someone else's authorship. In the EU and UK the exceptions are narrower. The CJEU's *Pelham II* ruling of 14 April 2026 requires pastiche to engage with the source in a "recognisable" creative dialogue ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/music-sampling-and-pastiche-cjeu-defines-the-scope-of-a-key-copyright-exception)), and the UK parody exception covers only fair dealing for "caricature, parody or pastiche" ([legislation.gov.uk, CDPA s.30A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/30A)). The EU quotation exception also requires the source to be credited, per Article 5(3)(d) as summarized from memory and not checked against the text, which a faceless re-upload that drops the creator's name cannot satisfy. Facecam removal does not affect copyright, but it creates separate attribution and publicity questions that were not researched. The conclusion from the sources is that a monetized, narration-stripped re-cut is **very likely infringing** under US law and outside the EU and UK exceptions. That is an inference from the cited law, not a legal opinion, and it needs a lawyer's review in each jurisdiction.

## Commentary is the only lane that pays without reposting

The routes with a policy basis are few. Original narration-led commentary, analysis or news that uses short clips to make a point sits inside YouTube's allowed list. Ad revenue follows, then sponsorships once an audience exists. Written licensing is the only route that pays for the footage itself, and the notes found no example of an approved request.

| Route | Policy basis | Income signal (third-party, unverified unless noted) | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original commentary or analysis with short clips | Inside YouTube's allowed list | Long-form gaming RPM about $1.20 to $4.50 | Audience build time; Content ID false positives; spoilers |
| Shorts as discovery | Original, under one minute when claims exist | Gaming Shorts RPM about $0.02 to $0.09 | Very little income per view |
| Sponsorship | Needs clean rights; brands may ask | Gaming CPM quotes range from $4 to $30 across sources | Needs an audience first |
| Creator codes or affiliates | Publisher program | No GTA 6 program found | None found |
| Clip campaigns | Rights come from the streamer; Rockstar commercial use unresolved | About $1 per 1,000 views (single SEO source) | Scams; rights disputes |
| Reposts or compilations | Fails reused-content rule even with permission | Not applicable | Channel-wide demonetization |

These figures come from SEO and tool blogs that disagree with each other. For example, one faceless-channel guide says creators keep about 55% of long-form ad revenue, while the Shorts pool gives creators 45% of their allocation, and both are third-party. The gap in per-view income is still large even on the notes' own estimates, where Shorts pay often more than ten times less per view than long-form. Shorts therefore work as discovery, and long-form carries the income. Shorts earnings also depend on the 2027 Shorts threshold of 10 million qualified views, which a new channel will not reach early, so the channel needs long-form watch hours from the start. Clip campaigns look attractive, but a streamer who holds no rights to GTA cannot grant them, and Rockstar's fan policy does not cover commercial use. The notes also report a secondary claim that YouTube's September 2026 update makes graphic gameplay in the first seven seconds ad-ineligible ([bige.co](https://bige.co/blogs/resources/youtube-just-made-a-huge-monetization-change-for-gaming-creators)). That rule is **unverified** against YouTube's own policy page.

## Reposting channels get demonetized, and the best-documented GTA case was not Rockstar

No named gaming repost channel with a primary-source record of termination or strike was found. The clearest recent event was an automated wave of claims on 22 November 2026. Colombian musician Frank "El Santo" streamed GTA 6, and automated claims then hit streamers playing the game, including theRadBrad (14.1 million subscribers) and HollowPoiint (5.2 million). [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/11/25/gta-6-streams-hit-with-takedowns-over-in-game-reggaeton-song/) reports that the claims "apparently had nothing to do with Rockstar" and may have come from YouTube's Content ID system matching gameplay against a channel that hosts official music. El Santo said he was "not filing these claims manually" and urged creators to dispute them ([Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/youtube/musician-responds-as-countless-youtubers-hit-with-copyright-strikes-on-gta-6-videos-3430646/)). The lesson is that automated claims can hit legitimate GTA content, so any channel built on GTA footage carries that exposure.

Take-Two does enforce against modding and leak content. It issued a takedown on a YouTube video of a GTA V mod map in 2025 ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2025/04/02/take-two-dmcas-video-of-gta5-mod-to-for-gta6-map-content/)), and a GTA IV mod channel was taken down in January 2025 ([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/309195/20250124/take-two-takes-down-gta-modders-youtube-channel-creators-push-vice-city-next-gen-edition.htm)). These cases concern mods and leaks, not commentary channels. A GTA V AI mod was removed from Nexus Mods after a Take-Two strike, and its creator says the strike blocks monetization for three months ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/gaming/pc-gaming/gta-5-ai-powered-mod-removed-from-nexus-mods-following-take-two-copyright-strike-modder-claims)), but that is the creator's own claim. Self-reported reports of channels losing most of their revenue after reused-content demonetization also exist, but they are unverified and skew toward channels that recovered by moving to commentary. YouTube's own rules say that creating new channels to evade a demonetization can get all of them terminated ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)), so the platform is the enforcement mechanism that matters most for reposters.

## Conclusion: the most viable path

The most viable path is a faceless analysis and commentary channel that is clearly the creator's own work. Every video should be built around an original script, the creator's narration, and original graphics, with clips used only where a specific point needs them, kept short, and credited. Shorts should stay under one minute, and the channel should avoid cutscene compilations, spoilers and leaks, which Rockstar's policy singles out. This path needs no game ownership and no gameplay recording, and it avoids the reposting pattern that YouTube's channel-wide rule punishes.

The uncomfortable finding is that even the lawful version uses other people's footage, so the lane narrows over time. Income should come first from long-form ads, then sponsorships once watch hours exist, and a written license from Take-Two (via copyright@take2games.com, which takes case-by-case requests) is the only way to pay for specific clips. The notes did not test whether official Rockstar trailers or press images can be used this way, and that is the most useful check to run before publishing. Launch-window demand fades quickly, so the content should be built to outlast the release.

Revenue will be slow. No verified revenue data exists for a GTA commentary channel, and the 2027 thresholds make the ad path longer than current guides assume. Treat every number here as an estimate, confirm YouTube's current terms in YouTube Studio, and get a lawyer's review before monetizing any video that uses Rockstar footage.
