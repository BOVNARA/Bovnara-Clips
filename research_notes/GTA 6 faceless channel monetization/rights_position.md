# Rights position: re-publishing edited third-party gameplay (narration and facecam removed) for a monetized faceless channel

> **Not legal advice.** This is research notes for a business decision. Have a qualified lawyer in each relevant jurisdiction review any plan before publishing or monetizing. Current as of 2026-11-26.
>
> Labels used below: **[Settled]** = stable, well-established law or a clear published policy. **[Contested]** = depends on case-by-case application or an open interpretive question. **[Unverified]** = from background knowledge, not checked against a source in this session.
>

## Key Question 1: Would a cut of another creator's gameplay with narration removed be transformative under US fair use?

### Takeaway
Probably not, on current law. The transformative argument in reaction and commentary cases rests on the new user's own critical or commentary content. Removing the original creator's narration and adding nothing back leaves a re-cut of the same footage for the same entertainment purpose, which is the situation Warhol treats as weak for the first factor. Commentary and reaction uses can still qualify; a pure re-cut is the weakest version of the argument.

### Cited Findings
- The US fair use factors are the statutory four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. 107; Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) treats transformation as central, and commercial character is not presumptively disqualifying. [Settled; Campbell and §107 from background knowledge, not fetched]
- Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) narrowed the first factor. The Court held that the inquiry asks whether the use has a further purpose or different character, that this is a matter of degree, and that where the original and the secondary use share the same or highly similar purposes and the use is commercial, the first factor is likely to weigh against fair use absent some other justification. — [Jenner client alert](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/client-alert-putting-the-use-back-in-fair-use-the-supreme-court-decides-andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith); [Miller Canfield summary](https://www.millercanfield.com/resources-SCOTUS-Rules-Against-Andy-Warhol-Foundation-Fair-Use.html). The Court's exact wording was not checked against the opinion text. [Settled as to holding; exact quotations Unverified]
- In Hosseinzadeh v. Klein, 276 F. Supp. 3d 34 (S.D.N.Y. 2017), a 14-minute reaction video using about three minutes of a five-minute skit was held fair use as a matter of law. The court weighed the first factor "heavily" in the defendants' favor because the video was "quintessential criticism and comment," and it found the clips "plainly necessary" for that commentary. — [US Copyright Office fair use summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/hosseinzadeh-klein-sdny2017.pdf); [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hosseinzadeh_v._Klein)
- The same court stressed that it was not ruling that all reaction videos are fair use. — [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hosseinzadeh_v._Klein)
- Secondary commentary on the Jukin Media v. Ray William Johnson ("Equals Three") litigation reports that 18 of 19 clips were held fair use in the episodes at issue. In one episode, the commentary was held not to add anything to or criticize the clip, so that episode was not fair use. The reporter citation was not verified. — [Odin Law summary](https://odinlaw.com/reaction-videos-fair-use-copyright-law/) [Unverified as to case citation]

### Inferences
- Commentary versus re-cut: a version that keeps the original creator's narration, or adds genuine criticism, reaction or new commentary, has a credible first-factor argument under Hosseinzadeh. A version with the narration stripped and nothing substituted looks like a re-cut or repackage of the footage. Under Warhol, that is a same-purpose use that is commercial, which cuts against fair use.
- Removing narration does not just strip commentary. It also removes the most obvious transformative element that the original creator supplied. The result is a copy of the creator's cut, not a new work.
- Because the copied footage in a re-cut is substantially the same as the source, the third factor (amount and substantiality) also weighs against the re-publisher. Hosseinzadeh treated clip use as acceptable only because it was "necessary" to the commentary. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No post-Warhol US decision was found that addresses a narration-stripped gameplay re-cut specifically. Search for one was not exhaustive.
- The exact wording of the Warhol majority opinion was not checked against the opinion itself.

## Key Question 2: Who owns the rights in the underlying gameplay footage: the publisher, the video creator, or both?

### Takeaway
Two layers of rights exist, and a re-publisher needs to clear both. The publisher (Take-Two / Rockstar) owns the copyright in the game and in the in-game audiovisual output. The video creator owns the copyright in their own added authorship (narration, facecam, overlays, editing choices and selection). Whether pure gameplay recordings carry independent player authorship is contested. Rockstar's own policy asserts that the footage is its copyrighted material.

### Cited Findings
- Rockstar's policy states that "Take-Two Interactive reserves the right to have its copyrighted material taken down at any time." — [Rockstar Support: Policy on posting copyrighted Rockstar Games material (last updated 2025-01-10)](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/7bNaeoMFTV0iUDGhStTXvz/policy-on-posting-copyrighted-rockstar-games-material) [Settled as a statement of publisher position]
- The same policy says Take-Two "does not object" to fans using material for non-commercial purposes that do not intentionally spoil the plot. It defines non-commercial as not making money through the footage and not using it as part of a promotion. [Rockstar Support, same page] [Settled as policy; the policy is a revocable tolerance, not a licence]
- The policy specifically calls out: "an edited combination of cutscenes and/or in-game plot dialogue" as a spoiler takedown; "isolated videos of the game's cinematics" as removable; and "in-game entertainment" such as TV shows or comedy performances posted in isolation as removable. It also prohibits "any posting of in-game footage from leaked copies of the game prior to its official release date." [Rockstar Support, same page]
- The policy says it "does not apply to the exploitation of Rockstar IP for film, TV, or streaming distribution; in music videos; advertisements; or publication in books, magazines, or digital publishing," and that licensing requests can be made case by case. [Rockstar Support, same page]
- A secondary guide (bo3.gg, 2026-11-11) reports that Rockstar has not issued a GTA 6-specific creator policy and that the January 2025 general policy applies. [bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/games/articles/gta-6-content-creator-rules-spoilers-streaming-and-youtube-monetization) [Unverified as to currency; confirm against Rockstar directly]
- The same secondary guide states that another creator's permission "does not grant rights to Rockstar's game content." [bo3.gg] [Settled principle, consistent with the two-layer analysis]

### Inferences
- The publisher's rights cover the game footage. The original creator's rights cover what they added. Both must be cleared, or the use must fall within an exception, to re-publish an edited version.
- Removing the narration and facecam does not remove the original creator's rights. The creator's edit (cut selection, pacing, sequencing, overlays) is still a copy of their authorship, even when the commentary is gone. [Inference]
- Whether a player's gameplay recording has its own protectable authorship is contested. The publisher's position appears to be that footage is its copyrighted material, which is a weaker argument for the re-publisher's side.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source was found on how the US Copyright Office or courts treat the copyright status of gameplay recordings as such.
- Rockstar's Terms of Service and EULA were not fetched in this session. They may restrict commercial use or the transfer of rights to user content. These terms should be checked.
- Whether the creator's narration and facecam are separately copyrightable works (and who owns them if the creator is an employee or contractor) was not researched.

## Key Question 3: How do EU and UK rules differ?

### Takeaway
Neither the EU nor the UK has a general US-style fair use. Each has a closed list of exceptions, and each applies to a specific purpose. Quotation or criticism exceptions, and the new caricature, parody and pastiche exceptions, are the relevant routes. The Court of Justice's April 2026 Pelham II ruling sets the current EU standard for pastiche, and it significantly narrows what counts. A pure re-cut of gameplay is unlikely to qualify under either the EU or UK exceptions. The UK parody exception is probably the most flexible, but it still requires a parody or similar purpose.

### Cited Findings
- **UK CDPA s.30A** (caricature, parody or pastiche): "Fair dealing with a work for the purposes of caricature, parody or pastiche does not infringe copyright in the work." Any contract term that prevents an act permitted by s.30A is unenforceable. In force since 1 October 2014. — [legislation.gov.uk, CDPA 1988 s.30A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/30A) [Settled, text fetched]
- **UK s.30(1)** (criticism, review, quotation) requires fair dealing for a work that has been made available to the public, with sufficient acknowledgement. [Unverified; s.30 not fetched]
- **CJEU Case C-590/23 (Pelham II), judgment of 14 April 2026, ECLI:EU:C:2026:290**: Article 5(3)(k) of Directive 2001/29 (pastiche) "does not have a catch-all nature" and "covers creations which evoke one or more existing works, while being noticeably different from them, and which use ... some of those works' characteristic elements protected by copyright, in order to engage with those works in an artistic or creative dialogue that is recognisable as such." Forms include overt stylistic imitation, tribute, and "humorous or critical engagement." Hidden imitation and plagiarism fall outside it. — [CJEU judgment record, ipcuria.eu](https://ipcuria.eu/case?reference=C-590/23); [CJEU press release No 50/26](https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2026-04/cp260050en.pdf); [GT Law summary](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/cjeu-clarifies-the-pastiche-exception-under-eu-copyright-law); [Jones Day summary](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/music-sampling-and-pastiche-cjeu-defines-the-scope-of-a-key-copyright-exception) [Settled as to holding; the case concerned music sampling]
- Jones Day reports the ruling is expected to matter for audiovisual and advertising uses as well as music. That is commentary on the ruling's reach, not a holding about video. — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/music-sampling-and-pastiche-cjeu-defines-the-scope-of-a-key-copyright-exception) [Contested as to extension]
- **Directive 2001/29 Article 5(3)(d)** (quotation for criticism or review): requires the work to have been lawfully made available, the source (including author's name) to be indicated unless impossible, use to be "in accordance with fair practice," and the extent to be "required by the specific purpose." [Unverified; text not fetched]
- Pelham I (C-476/17, 2019) confirmed that the Court looks for a fair balance with freedom of expression and the arts (Articles 11 and 13 of the Charter). [Settled; cited in the Pelham II judgment excerpt]

### Inferences
- Under Pelham II, a faceless compilation with narration removed is unlikely to be "pastiche," because it does not engage in a "recognisable" creative dialogue with the source. Only a version with real critical or humorous engagement would fit. [Inference]
- Under Article 5(3)(d), the source must be credited. A faceless re-upload that strips the original creator's name is in tension with this requirement, even where the quotation argument is otherwise available. [Inference]
- UK s.30 and s.30A require that the purpose be criticism, review, quotation, caricature, parody or pastiche. A repackage does not obviously meet any of these. [Inference]
- The UK also gives authors moral rights in their works, including the right not to have a work subjected to "derogatory treatment" (CDPA s.80) and the right to be identified (s.77). Heavy editing of a creator's video may engage these, though this was not researched. [Unverified]

### Gaps
- No EU or UK case was found that specifically addresses re-edited third-party gameplay compilations.
- Member-state variation in the EU (for example Germany's §51a UrhG, which implements the pastiche exception) was not compared in detail.
- The UK fair dealing position for commercial use was not checked against a current source.

## Key Question 4: Does monetization weigh against fair use, and how does market harm (factor four) apply?

### Takeaway
Yes, on current US law. Warhol treats commercial character as weighing against fair use where purposes overlap. Monetization is also the trigger for the publisher's policy: Rockstar treats any money earned from footage, or use in a promotion, as commercial and outside its tolerance. Market substitution is the main harm argument. A re-cut that could serve as a substitute for the original video, or for the creator's own channel, is the strongest form of harm.

### Cited Findings
- Warhol: where a secondary use is commercial and shares the same or highly similar purpose as the original, the first factor is likely to weigh against fair use. [Jenner](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/client-alert-putting-the-use-back-in-fair-use-the-supreme-court-decides-andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith) [Settled as holding]
- Hosseinzadeh found factor four favored the defendants because their video did "not serve as a market substitute" for the original skit, since it "transforms" the skit into commentary. [US Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/hosseinzadeh-klein-sdny2017.pdf) [Settled as to that case]
- Rockstar's policy defines commercial use as making money through the footage, or using it in a product or service promotion. It says advertising, sponsored, and pay-per-view footage "will be presumed to be in violation." That text appears in the bo3.gg secondary summary; the Rockstar page as fetched gives the "does not object" and "non-commercial" wording. [Rockstar Support](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/7bNaeoMFTV0iUDGhStTXvz/policy-on-posting-copyrighted-rockstar-games-material); [bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/games/articles/gta-6-content-creator-rules-spoilers-streaming-and-youtube-monetization) [Settled for the Rockstar wording as fetched; the "presumed to be in violation" line appears only in the secondary summary and should be verified]
- Platform-level monetization is a separate issue from copyright. YouTube's fair use page states that a claim "will keep you from monetizing the video, even if you only use a few seconds," and that Content ID cannot decide fair use. — [YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en) [Settled as to YouTube's stated process]
- A secondary report says YouTube updated its gaming-violence monetization rules in September 2026. Graphic gameplay in the first seven seconds makes a video ineligible for ads, and some categories (for example compilations focused on mass killings of NPCs) are ineligible throughout. — [bige.co](https://bige.co/blogs/resources/youtube-just-made-a-huge-monetization-change-for-gaming-creators?srsltid=AYCX9vJZIKnpuFX23RNW_GnHZDlkmly3GbwOjPWNeoes0xBGm2N96V8O); [bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/games/articles/gta-6-content-creator-rules-spoilers-streaming-and-youtube-monetization) [Unverified against YouTube's own policy page]

### Inferences
- Monetization weighs against fair use on both the first and fourth factors. It also makes the Rockstar tolerance unavailable, because the tolerance is expressly limited to non-commercial use. This means that once the channel earns money, the publisher's policy no longer protects it, even if the copyright analysis were otherwise uncertain. [Inference]
- Market harm is most likely where a faceless compilation of the creator's edit competes with that creator's own channel for views, which is the core commercial harm. [Inference]
- The platform side is a separate risk: ad eligibility can be lost even where copyright is not at issue. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No quantified data on market harm from faceless compilation channels was found.
- The September 2026 YouTube change was confirmed only in secondary sources.

## Key Question 5: Precedents and notable cases involving re-edited third-party gameplay or reaction and compilation channels

### Takeaway
The strongest precedent for reaction content is Hosseinzadeh v. Klein (S.D.N.Y. 2017), where commentary made the use fair. Nothing found in this session is a decision that approves a gameplay compilation with the commentary stripped out. Earlier game-specific authority (Galoob v. Nintendo) concerns derivative tools rather than re-publication of footage and is of limited use here.

### Cited Findings
- Hosseinzadeh v. Klein, 276 F. Supp. 3d 34 (S.D.N.Y. 2017): reaction video fair use as a matter of law; defendants' DMCA counter-notification upheld; §512(f) claim dismissed. — [US Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/hosseinzadeh-klein-sdny2017.pdf); [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hosseinzadeh_v._Klein) [Settled]
- Jukin Media v. Ray William Johnson ("Equals Three"): fair use found for most clips with commentary; not for an episode where commentary did not engage with the clip. — [Odin Law](https://odinlaw.com/reaction-videos-fair-use-copyright-law/) [Case citation Unverified]
- Lenz v. Universal Music (9th Cir. 2016) requires a copyright holder to consider fair use before sending a takedown notice. [Unverified; citation from background knowledge, not fetched] This matters because a takedown is the publisher's or creator's first enforcement route.
- Lewis Galoob Enterprises v. Nintendo (9th Cir. 1992) held that the Game Genie, which altered game display without copying the game's code or audiovisual content, did not infringe. Its reasoning turned on the absence of a fixed copy of the protected expression. [Unverified; background knowledge]. It is weaker authority for a re-cut that copies the footage directly.
- Secondary commentary (Vogele, Touro Law Review, "Where's the Fair Use?") argues that Let's Play and reaction takedowns frequently overreach and that long clips are commonly the problem. [Touro Law Review](https://digitalcommons.tourolaw.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2824&context=lawreview) [Snippet only; article not read in full]

### Inferences
- Across the reaction-video cases, courts have protected the new user's critical content and tied clip length to that commentary. A channel that removes the original creator's commentary and keeps only the footage moves away from the protected fact pattern. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No decision was found on a gaming compilation channel that re-publishes another creator's cut with narration removed.
- Court decisions on gameplay "let's play" or compilation content since Warhol were not searched exhaustively.

## Key Question 6: Does removing the creator's narration or facecam change the rights position?

### Takeaway
Removing narration makes the position worse. Removing the facecam does not solve the copyright problem, and it creates separate attribution and likeness concerns. Removing either element does not clear the publisher's rights or the original creator's authorship in their edit.

### Cited Findings
- Rockstar's policy addresses the footage and does not mention narration or facecam. Its guidance is about spoilers, cutscenes, and commercial use. [Rockstar Support](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/7bNaeoMFTV0iUDGhStTXvz/policy-on-posting-copyrighted-rockstar-games-material) [Settled as to what the policy covers]
- In Hosseinzadeh, the defendants' own commentary was the basis for the transformative purpose. [US Copyright Office summary] [Settled as to that case]
- UK s.30A and s.30 require the purpose to be parody or criticism. Narration stripped from a re-cut removes the most likely purpose claim. [legislation.gov.uk] [Settled as to statutory text]

### Inferences
- Narration is the creator's own copyrightable contribution and the main transformative element. Removing it strips the argument for fair use, and the result is an unaltered copy of the creator's cut. [Inference]
- The facecam is the creator's likeness and personal identity. Removing it does not change copyright ownership, but using a creator's video without attribution or consent may raise personality or publicity claims depending on jurisdiction. This was not researched. [Inference; Unverified]
- Removing narration and facecam does not change what is copied. It removes evidence of transformation and attribution, which makes the use look more like wholesale copying. [Inference]

### Gaps
- Personality, publicity and right-of-publicity claims were not researched for any jurisdiction.

## Overall bottom line (for the decision)
- Pure re-cut of gameplay with narration and facecam removed, monetized: **very likely infringing** under US law on the publisher's and original creator's rights, and **outside** the EU and UK exceptions. Monetization also removes the Rockstar tolerance. [Inference from sources above]
- Version that adds genuine original commentary, criticism, or reaction, with a short clip count and proper source credit: **a credible fair use argument** in the US under Hosseinzadeh, with EU and UK quotation or parody arguments depending on the content. [Inference]
- Check before any publication: source footage provenance (pre-release leaks are removed under Rockstar's policy), spoiler content, Rockstar's Terms of Service, and YouTube's current monetization rules.
